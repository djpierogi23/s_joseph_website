Exit strategy

Condominium Conversion

Converting a building to condominiums is one of the two ways a completed project realizes its value — and the one with the most regulatory process attached to it.

Condominium conversion turns a single building into individually owned and separately saleable units. It is how a developer converts a finished asset into unit-by-unit sales proceeds rather than holding it for rent. It is also a filing-heavy process governed by state regulators, and the timeline it adds to a project is frequently underestimated.

The offering plan is not paperwork at the end of the project. It is a document that should be contemplated from the moment the building is designed.

What the process involves

In both New York and New Jersey, converting to condominium ownership requires preparing and filing an offering plan — a disclosure document describing the building, the units, the budget, and the sponsor's obligations — and obtaining regulatory acceptance before units can be sold.

  • Offering plan preparation. The disclosure document itself, assembled with the sponsor's architect, engineer, and accountant.
  • Master deed and bylaws. The instruments that create the condominium regime and govern how it operates.
  • Regulatory filing and review. Submission to the state regulator, and the comment-and-response cycle that follows.
  • Sponsor representation. Advising the sponsor on its ongoing obligations through the sell-out period.
  • Unit closings. Closing individual unit sales, from the first through the last.

When to start

Earlier than most sponsors expect. Regulatory review takes months, and units cannot be sold under contract until the plan is accepted. A conversion contemplated during design and financing — rather than after Certificate of Occupancy — avoids the most common delay, which is discovering that the building as built does not match the condominium structure the sponsor intended.
